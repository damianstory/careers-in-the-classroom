## 2. GHGSat — Classroom story ( /examples/ghgsat )

- H1: "How can you find an invisible gas from space?"
- "GHGSat · Lists a Calgary office. Headquarters in Montreal."
- Fits: the three course lines above + link "Pick your class"
- Block "Use it in class":
  - ASK THE CLASS: "If a sensor detects no methane, does that prove none is present? What would you want to know about the instrument and the observation conditions?"
  - "Detecting a gas, estimating how much is escaping, and deciding what to do are different tasks. Instruments, analysis and the people interpreting results all contribute."
  - "Mindset it shows: Understand measurement limits before drawing conclusions."
- Large schematic (methane diagram, as in card 3, bigger) + caption "An illustrative connection between this unit and real work. Use it to support your classroom discussion." + "Schematic drawn for this page and checked against GHGSat’s technology description and Jervis et al. (2021). Not to scale. Not a GHGSat image."
- "The lesson, in the real world" — lead: "The physics of light lets a satellite about 500 km up measure a gas no one can see." Then 4 numbered steps (this IS a sequence):
  1 "Sunlight contains many wavelengths, including shortwave infrared light our eyes can’t see."
  2 "Methane absorbs some of that light at particular wavelengths, between about 1,630 and 1,675 nanometres."
  3 "The satellite’s spectrometer measures sunlight scattered back up from the ground. It sends out no light of its own."
  4 "Where methane is present, a little less light arrives at those wavelengths. Analysts combine that dip with wind data to estimate how fast gas is escaping."
  - "Our explanation · Source: GHGSat: Technology (opens in a new tab); Jervis et al. 2021, The GHGSat-D imaging spectrometer (Atmos. Meas. Tech.)"
- Meet the company: "GHGSat" / "Measuring methane emissions from space, and from aircraft." / link "Meet GHGSat"
- Who does this work?: Atmospheric science and remote sensing · Satellite and instrument engineering · Software and data · Business development and partnerships / link "See the roles"

- Use it in class — ASK THE CLASS: "What would you measure over several years to decide whether this intervention is helping? What comparison would make your conclusion more convincing?" / "Saving animals and restoring a population are connected but different goals. Scientists need evidence about what happens after release, not just a count of animals cared for." / "Mindset it shows: Evaluate outcomes beyond the most visible success metric."
- Schematic "From care to evidence" (a cycle — this IS a sequence): 01 Vulnerable young owls — A difficult early life stage / 02 Care through winter — Food, welfare and health checks / 03 Release in spring — Pairs return to prairie habitat / 04 Follow what happens — Survival, breeding and offspring / loop-back "↶ Evidence informs the next intervention" / "A release is a step. Recovery is the question."
  Caption: "An illustrative connection between population biology and conservation work." + "Illustrative process schematic created for this page from Wilder’s program and care-team descriptions. It shows no measured outcomes or guaranteed recovery."
- The lesson, in the real world — lead: "Supporting a vulnerable life stage is an intervention. Measuring recovery requires following what happens next."
  1 "Wilder temporarily brings vulnerable young burrowing owls into care through their first winter."
  2 "Animal-care teams provide daily care while veterinarians monitor health and body condition."
  3 "Adults are released in breeding pairs in southeastern Alberta, with supplementary food and monitoring."
  4 "To evaluate recovery, ask about survival and reproduction after release, over time and against a useful comparison. Counting released owls answers only part of that question."
  Our explanation · Source: Wilder Institute: burrowing owl program; Wilder Institute: owl care team (2023)
