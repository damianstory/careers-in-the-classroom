**Local note:** Calgary studio; the featured project is Dale Hodges Park in Calgary.

## Company tab — proposed copy

O2 is a planning and design practice with a Calgary studio. At Dale Hodges Park, it worked with other specialists to combine stormwater treatment, habitat and public space. Its project account describes marshes, wet meadows and landforms that guide water before it reaches the Bow River. This was collaborative work; O2 did not design every element alone.

**The problem we are exploring:** How can a park help clean stormwater before it reaches a river?

**Work context:** Designed marshes and wet meadows within a collaborative stormwater park project.

**Company evidence:** [Source 1](https://www.o2design.com/dale-hodges-park); [Source 2](https://www.o2design.com/contact-us); [Source 3](https://www.o2design.com/careers); [Source 4](https://www.o2design.com/s/O2_Junior-Landscape-Designer.pdf); [Source 5](https://engage.o2design.com/about.html)

## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A park can be part of a city’s water system.

1. Runoff carries water and suspended material from streets into drainage systems.

2. At Dale Hodges Park, water follows a designed route through a landscape rather than remaining hidden in pipes.

3. Slower flow can allow some suspended particles to settle. Different treatment stages address different parts of the problem.

4. The landscape also supports habitat and recreation. A useful design must account for these needs together.

**Discuss:** Would the clearest-looking water necessarily be the safest water?

**Try thinking it through:** Draw two fictional routes from a street drain to a river: one short and fast, one with space for settling. Mark where to sample and one habitat trade-off.

**Teacher note:** Clear water can still contain dissolved contaminants. This is a planning model, not a drinking-water treatment demonstration.

Consistency depends on checking what happened, not just repeating a recipe.

1. A roast changes a batch of green coffee through heating. Time and temperature matter, but they do not describe every property of the result.

**Discuss:** If two batches have the same recorded temperature history, what else would you want to measure?

## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A compact system still needs the right sequence, connections and access for maintenance.

1. Start with incoming-water characteristics and the required output, not the size of a shipping frame.

2. Choose compatible treatment stages and account for water, chemicals and residual streams.

3. Arrange equipment so connections, controls and maintenance access can work within the available space.

4. Test the integrated system against the requirement. A compact layout is useful only if the treatment and operation also work.

**Discuss:** What could go wrong if a designer only tries to make the equipment smaller?

**Try thinking it through:** Arrange four fictional modules on a paper floor plan: feed tank, treatment, output check and residual storage. Draw flows and leave maintenance access. Explain one trade-off when the footprint is reduced.

### Process engineering

Turn a useful transformation into a workable sequence of equipment and operating steps. Process engineers consider what enters, what leaves and what conditions the system must maintain.

**Typical tasks:**

- Draw a flow of materials and energy through the system.
- Compare operating conditions and equipment options.
- Use trial results to revise the process and communicate constraints.

**Works with:** Laboratory staff, operators, mechanical designers and controls specialists.

**Classroom connection:** Conservation of matter and energy connects classroom calculations to process decisions.

**Company evidence:** ClearBakk describes process design and engineering services.

### Mechanical design and prototyping

Translate a need into a component or machine that can be built and tested. Designers use dimensions, material properties and constraints to evaluate alternatives.

**Typical tasks:**

- Create and revise drawings or a digital model.
- Choose materials and dimensions for the intended conditions.
- Compare the prototype with its requirements and refine the design.

**Works with:** Machinists, fabricators, test staff and customers.

**Classroom connection:** Geometry, forces and material properties shape real design trade-offs.

**Company evidence:** Modular integration supports mechanical layout work; generic profile.

### Instrumentation and controls

Keep a process measurable and responsive. Controls work connects sensors, decisions and actuators so the system can respond when conditions change.

**Typical tasks:**

- Check whether sensors are reading reliably.
- Compare measurements with the intended operating range.
- Test how the system responds to a change or fault.

**Works with:** Operators, electricians and process engineers.

**Classroom connection:** Feedback uses a measured result to inform the next action.

**Company evidence:** Controls connection is editorial to integrated equipment; not an employer-listed vacancy.
