## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A useful water plan asks what might change, not just how much water is available today.

1. Describe the sources and uses of water in the system.

2. Build more than one scenario for future demand and available supply.

3. Compare options such as reducing demand or changing which water source serves a use. Reuse requires the right treatment and controls.

4. Choose actions that can be revised as monitoring and circumstances change. A plan should state uncertainty rather than hide it.

**Discuss:** Would you choose the option that is best in one forecast or useful across several forecasts?

**Try thinking it through:** Use fictional annual demand of 100 units. Compare a 10-unit demand reduction under supplies of 120, 100 and 80 units. Identify the remaining deficit and one assumption the arithmetic leaves out.

**Teacher note:** Units are fictional. Real planning includes seasonality, ecosystems, rights, infrastructure and water quality; subtraction alone is not a water-security assessment.

**Course connection:** Science 10 — water in environmental systems and inquiry; precise outcome fit provisional. Scenario analysis is an extension, not a verified policy outcome.

## Roles tab — proposed copy

These profiles explain kinds of work, not promises of a job. Company links are qualified separately.

### Water-resource planning

Compare water availability, demand and possible responses over time.

**Typical tasks:**

- Organize supply, use and monitoring information.
- Test alternative assumptions in scenarios.
- Explain trade-offs, uncertainty and priorities.

**Works with:** Hydrologists, engineers, policy specialists and communities.

**Classroom connection:** A model is a way to test assumptions, not a guarantee about the future.

**Company evidence:** City roadmap and company team page support water-planning work.

### Environmental field assessment

Collect and interpret evidence about a place before recommending an action.

**Typical tasks:**

- Plan observations or samples around a clear question.
- Record locations, methods and conditions consistently.
- Explain the limits of the evidence and the next useful check.

**Works with:** Laboratory staff, engineers and project planners.

**Classroom connection:** A result has meaning only in the context of how and where it was collected.

**Company evidence:** Company lists environmental scientists; no specific vacancy or roadmap assignment is asserted.

### Planning communication and engagement

Help people with different needs understand and contribute to a planning process.

**Typical tasks:**

- Explain scenarios and technical options in accessible language.
- Gather and document questions and priorities.
- Make clear what the evidence supports and where choices remain.

**Works with:** Technical teams, decision-makers and community participants.

**Classroom connection:** A number can inform a decision without deciding whose needs take priority.

**Company evidence:** Company lists facilitators, communicators and policy advisers; tasks are editorial.
