## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A familiar product becomes a different problem when it has to be repeated at scale.

1. Define what consistency means for the product: size, appearance and other agreed properties may all matter.

2. Keep track of ingredients, processing conditions and batch identity.

3. Check samples from more than one point in a batch; an average can conceal individual differences.

4. Investigate a pattern and test one change at a time before deciding whether the process improved.

**Discuss:** Can two batches have the same average and still differ in quality?

**Try thinking it through:** Compare fictional piece masses: batch A is 49, 50, 50, 51 g; batch B is 40, 50, 50, 60 g. Both average 50 g. Compare spread and explain why an average alone is insufficient.

**Teacher note:** The figures are invented and no company acceptance limits are implied. Mass comparison does not establish food safety.

**Course connection:** Science 10 — controlled investigation and measurement; statistics and food production are extensions. Do not present recipe scale-up as chemical stoichiometry without a reaction model.

## Roles tab — proposed copy

These profiles explain kinds of work, not promises of a job. Company links are qualified separately.

### Commercial baking

Produce baked goods through a controlled sequence of preparation, processing and handling.

**Typical tasks:**

- Follow recipes and production schedules.
- Use and check production equipment within trained procedures.
- Observe product consistency and communicate defects or process variation.

**Works with:** Production staff, quality teams and maintenance workers.

**Classroom connection:** Ratios, timing and temperature matter, but quality must still be checked.

**Company evidence:** Company describes manufacturing and production teams; no current vacancy claimed.

### Quality and traceability

Make it possible to explain how a result or product was produced. Quality work checks records, methods and acceptance criteria so variation can be investigated rather than hidden.

**Typical tasks:**

- Check that materials and samples have traceable identities.
- Compare recorded results with defined acceptance criteria.
- Investigate deviations and document what changed.

**Works with:** Production, laboratory, engineering and customer teams.

**Classroom connection:** Repeated results need both measurements and records of the conditions that produced them.

**Company evidence:** Company describes quality assurance; no named job description captured.

### Ingredient and formulation science

Investigate how ingredients behave in a product. This work connects a material’s composition to properties such as texture, stability or performance in a mixture.

**Typical tasks:**

- Choose a property to compare across formulations.
- Prepare controlled trials and observe changes over time.
- Explain the trade-offs between ingredients and processing conditions.

**Works with:** Biochemists, laboratory technicians and product-development teams.

**Classroom connection:** Molecules and mixtures help explain why two ingredients can behave differently.

**Company evidence:** Company describes product development; no particular scientist role or degree requirement is asserted.
