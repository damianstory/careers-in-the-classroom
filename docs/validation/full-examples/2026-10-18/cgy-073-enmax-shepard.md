## Company

ENMAX operates Shepard Energy Centre, a combined-cycle natural-gas generating facility in southeast Calgary, through a joint venture with Capital Power. Its generation information describes gas turbines and a steam turbine. The classroom story follows the energy: exhaust heat from the first stage helps produce additional electricity in the next. It does not present this as zero-emission or loss-free generation.

**Problem:** Why use the heat left over from making electricity to make more electricity?

**Offering:** Generate power with gas turbines and recover exhaust heat to drive a steam turbine.

**Who this serves:** People and organizations supplied by Alberta's electrical grid.

**Location boundary:** operating power station in Calgary. Company identifies Shepard Energy Centre in southeast Calgary; joint venture with Capital Power, operated by ENMAX.

**Original company/work sources:** [CGY-073-S01](https://www.enmax.com/generation-and-wires/generation/natural-gas-fuelled) · [CGY-073-S02](https://www.enmax.com/generation-and-wires/generation) · [CGY-073-S03](https://www.enmax.com/careers/) · [CGY-073-S04](https://alis.alberta.ca/occinfo/occupations-in-alberta/occupation-profiles/power-engineer/)

## How it works

1. Natural gas supplies energy to a gas-turbine stage, which drives electrical generation.
2. Hot exhaust still carries energy after that first stage.
3. Heat recovery uses some of that exhaust energy to make steam for another turbine and generator.
4. Additional electrical output comes from the same initial energy supply, but losses and heat rejection remain. A complete energy account must include them.

The explanations above are a classroom model grounded in the company/work sources and curriculum source, not a proprietary process specification.

## Classroom conversation

Why does adding a second generating stage not mean that the plant has created extra energy?

**Activity:** Use a fictional energy budget: input 100 units; first electrical output 35; second electrical output 20; remaining 45 to other transfers. Compare 35% and 55% model efficiencies and explain where the second stage's input comes from.

**Teacher note:** The numbers are invented and are not Shepard performance data. Distinguish energy from power; do not use MW as if it were an amount of energy. A power engineer is an operational profession with its own certification path, distinct from professional engineering registration.

## Roles

Company establishes generation operations; Alberta ALIS supports general power-engineer duties. Specific work at Shepard is interpreted rather than sourced to an individual requisition. General tasks below are editorial explanations, not a current staff roster or live job advertisements.

### Power engineering and plant operations

Operate and monitor equipment that produces or transfers thermal and electrical energy.

- Read operating measurements and compare them with procedures.
- Coordinate inspections and maintenance with other teams.
- Track an abnormal condition and respond through established operating processes.

**Works with:** Control-room teams, Maintenance staff, Engineers.

**Classroom connection:** Energy diagrams become real operating systems with constraints and losses.

**Related routes in this draft:** SAIT — Power Engineering Technology. These are connections to explore, not employer hiring requirements.

### Instrumentation and control

Help a system turn physical conditions into dependable readings and actions.

- Choose a measurement point and check the instrument's range.
- Compare readings with a reference and investigate missing or unexpected values.
- Use evidence to adjust a control or recommend a closer inspection.

**Works with:** Operators, Engineers, Maintenance teams.

**Classroom connection:** A reading depends on where and how it was measured, not just the number displayed.

**Related routes in this draft:** SAIT — Instrumentation Engineering Technology. These are connections to explore, not employer hiring requirements.

### Mechanical design and analysis

Turn a performance need into a component or system that can be built and checked.

- Translate the need into dimensions, loads and operating conditions.
- Compare drawings or models and explain a design trade-off.
- Work with manufacturing and test teams when a prototype behaves differently from the model.

**Works with:** Manufacturing staff, Test technicians, Customers.

**Classroom connection:** A model is useful when its assumptions are clear and measurements can challenge it.

**Related routes in this draft:** University of Calgary — Mechanical Engineering. These are connections to explore, not employer hiring requirements.
