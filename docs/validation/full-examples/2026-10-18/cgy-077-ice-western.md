## How it works

1. Warm exhaust air leaves a space while colder outdoor air enters in a winter example.
2. A coil in the exhaust path transfers some heat to a circulating fluid.
3. The fluid carries energy to a coil in the incoming-air path; the two air streams remain separate in this arrangement.
4. Fans and the circulation system also require energy. Recovery can reduce part of the heating demand without recovering every unit of heat.

The explanations above are a classroom model grounded in the company/work sources and curriculum source, not a proprietary process specification.

## Classroom conversation

Why might a designer choose separate air streams even if a simpler arrangement seemed easier to build?

**Activity:** Sketch exhaust at 20°C and outdoor air at −10°C, without assigning an output temperature. Mark energy-flow directions and list the measurements needed to estimate recovery. Identify why flow rates matter as well as temperature.

**Teacher note:** This is a run-around heat-recovery model, not every type of HVAC system. Do not depict dirty exhaust mixing directly with supply air or claim that a heat exchanger is a refrigerator. No glycol concentrations or equipment sizing instructions are needed.
