## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A useful separation must work again and again.

An illustrative connection between classroom ideas and sourced work.

1. A brine contains lithium alongside other dissolved substances. Its composition affects how a separation must be designed.

2. Summit describes columns containing a sorbent that takes up lithium, followed by a step that releases lithium into another stream.

3. Columns operate through repeated stages. Testing considers not only one successful cycle but also performance as the material is reused.

4. Compare selectivity, recovery, water use and durability together. A result from one brine or test condition is not automatically transferable to every resource.

**Discuss:** Would you choose a material that captures more lithium once, or one that performs consistently for many cycles?

**Try thinking it through:** Give students fictional recovery values over five cycles for two sorbents. Ask which additional measurements would be needed before choosing one for a real process.

**Teacher note:** Use the data only as an illustrative comparison. Do not claim the exercise reveals Summit’s proprietary formulation or measured performance.

**Subject connection:** Chemistry: solutions, separation and repeated-cycle testing. Distinct from E3 through the focus on sorbent reuse; precise outcomes remain provisional.

**Evidence distinction:** Company process statements come from the sources above. The teaching interpretation, fictional activity and discussion are editorial. No fictional values are company measurements.

## Roles tab — proposed copy

The profiles explain kinds of work. The company-evidence note under each profile states how strongly it is linked to this organization. None of these profiles is a promise of a vacancy.

### Materials research and testing

Investigate whether a material improves a product for a defined purpose. A useful comparison separates the effect of the added material from other changes in the test.

Typical tasks:

- Prepare comparable samples with and without an additive.
- Measure a relevant property under consistent conditions.
- Explain performance changes alongside uncertainty and practical constraints.

**Works with:** Chemists, process engineers and product developers.

**Classroom connection:** Stronger, lighter and more conductive are different properties requiring different tests.

**Company evidence:** Calgary sorbent R&D and pilot manufacturing are explicitly stated.

### Process engineering

Turn a useful transformation into a workable sequence of equipment and operating steps. Process engineers consider what enters, what leaves and what conditions the system must maintain.

Typical tasks:

- Draw a flow of materials and energy through the system.
- Compare operating conditions and equipment options.
- Use trial results to revise the process and communicate constraints.

**Works with:** Laboratory staff, operators, mechanical designers and controls specialists.

**Classroom connection:** Conservation of matter and energy connects classroom calculations to process decisions.

**Company evidence:** System engineering and process modeling are stated capabilities.

### Instrumentation and controls

Keep a process measurable and responsive. Controls work connects sensors, decisions and actuators so the system can respond when conditions change.

Typical tasks:

- Check whether sensors are reading reliably.
- Compare measurements with the intended operating range.
- Test how the system responds to a change or fault.

**Works with:** Operators, electricians and process engineers.

**Classroom connection:** Feedback uses a measured result to inform the next action.

**Company evidence:** Repeated column operation and modeling support a related controls work area; a specific title is not verified.
