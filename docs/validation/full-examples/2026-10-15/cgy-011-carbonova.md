## Example card

**How can carbon-containing gases become useful solid materials?**

Carbon atoms can become part of a different material.

**Local note:** Calgary pilot and demonstration-project evidence; the larger demonstration unit is not described here as commissioned.

## Company tab — proposed copy

Carbonova develops a catalytic process that uses carbon-containing gases to make carbon nanofibres. Emissions Reduction Alberta documents Calgary pilot work and a separate commercial-demonstration project. The example focuses on converting feedstocks and testing a material’s usefulness, rather than assuming that using an emission stream automatically delivers a net climate benefit.

**The problem:** Can a carbon-containing gas become an additive with useful material properties?

**What it offers:** Carbon nanofibre technology and material development for applications such as composites and construction materials.

**Who uses the work:** Material and product developers evaluating additives for a specific performance requirement.

**Evidence:** [Company technology and development status](https://carbonova.com/); [Active Calgary demonstration project](https://www.eralberta.ca/projects/details/carbon-nanofiber-commerical-demonstration/); [Completed CREATE process project](https://www.eralberta.ca/projects/details/carbon-reforming-to-economic-additives-for-transitioning-into-emission-less-era/)

## Classroom tab — proposed copy

Carbon atoms can become part of a different material.

An illustrative connection between classroom ideas and sourced work.

1. Carbon-containing feedstocks supply atoms that can become part of a solid product through a sequence of chemical processes.

2. ERA’s project account describes reforming followed by a carbonization reactor that produces solid carbon material.

3. The resulting nanofibres can be tested as additives. Compare a material with and without the additive under the same conditions.

4. Also examine the inputs, energy and remaining outputs. A useful product and a lower overall environmental footprint are related but separate claims.

**Discuss:** What would you measure to decide whether a new additive improves both performance and environmental impact?

**Try thinking it through:** Provide fictional strength-test results for identical samples with two additive amounts. Ask students to compare variation, identify a control and list the additional evidence required for an environmental claim.

**Teacher note:** A stronger sample alone cannot establish lower lifecycle emissions. No claim is made that these invented values are Carbonova test results.

**Subject connection:** Chemistry: reactions and conservation of matter; Physics: material properties; environmental evaluation. Curriculum mapping remains provisional.

**Evidence distinction:** Company process statements come from the sources above. The teaching interpretation, fictional activity and discussion are editorial. No fictional values are company measurements.

## Roles tab — proposed copy

The profiles explain kinds of work. The company-evidence note under each profile states how strongly it is linked to this organization. None of these profiles is a promise of a vacancy.

### Process engineering

Turn a useful transformation into a workable sequence of equipment and operating steps. Process engineers consider what enters, what leaves and what conditions the system must maintain.

Typical tasks:

- Draw a flow of materials and energy through the system.
- Compare operating conditions and equipment options.
- Use trial results to revise the process and communicate constraints.

**Works with:** Laboratory staff, operators, mechanical designers and controls specialists.

**Classroom connection:** Conservation of matter and energy connects classroom calculations to process decisions.

**Company evidence:** Pilot and scale-up work are documented by ERA; role tasks are editorial.

### Materials research and testing

Investigate whether a material improves a product for a defined purpose. A useful comparison separates the effect of the added material from other changes in the test.

Typical tasks:

- Prepare comparable samples with and without an additive.
- Measure a relevant property under consistent conditions.
- Explain performance changes alongside uncertainty and practical constraints.

**Works with:** Chemists, process engineers and product developers.

**Classroom connection:** Stronger, lighter and more conductive are different properties requiring different tests.

**Company evidence:** Customer material assessment and composite testing are supported by project descriptions.

### Laboratory analysis

Make measurements that other people can trust. A laboratory worker prepares samples, uses a method consistently and records enough detail for someone else to interpret the result.

Typical tasks:

- Prepare and label samples so their identity is preserved.
- Run comparisons or reference samples to check the method.
- Record results, units and limitations before sharing findings.

**Works with:** Scientists, process engineers and quality teams.

**Classroom connection:** A repeatable method and a fair comparison matter as much as a single impressive number.

**Company evidence:** Measurement and testing are connected work areas, not a verified named vacancy.
