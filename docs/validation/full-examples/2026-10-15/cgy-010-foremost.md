## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A heavy machine needs the ground to support it.

An illustrative connection between classroom ideas and sourced work.

1. A vehicle transfers its weight to the ground through the areas in contact with it.

2. For the same force, spreading contact over a larger area lowers average pressure. Tracks and wide tires illustrate different approaches.

3. Ground conditions, traction, load and terrain also affect movement. Average pressure alone cannot predict whether a vehicle will pass.

4. Designers and manufacturing teams must balance mobility with strength, stability, transport and service needs.

**Discuss:** Would doubling the contact area guarantee that a vehicle never sinks?

**Try thinking it through:** Use fictional loads and contact areas to compare average pressures. Then introduce uneven ground and ask which assumptions the simple calculation leaves out.

**Teacher note:** Keep the same force units and area units. This is a simplified model, not a performance claim for any Foremost vehicle.

**Subject connection:** Physics: force, pressure and simplified models; design and trades. Formal curriculum fit remains provisional.

**Evidence distinction:** Company process statements come from the sources above. The teaching interpretation, fictional activity and discussion are editorial. No fictional values are company measurements.

## Roles tab — proposed copy

The profiles explain kinds of work. The company-evidence note under each profile states how strongly it is linked to this organization. None of these profiles is a promise of a vacancy.

### Mechanical design and prototyping

Translate a need into a component or machine that can be built and tested. Designers use dimensions, material properties and constraints to evaluate alternatives.

Typical tasks:

- Create and revise drawings or a digital model.
- Choose materials and dimensions for the intended conditions.
- Compare the prototype with its requirements and refine the design.

**Works with:** Machinists, fabricators, test staff and customers.

**Classroom connection:** Geometry, forces and material properties shape real design trade-offs.

**Company evidence:** Company manufacturing and equipment capabilities establish design-related work; individual assignments are not confirmed.

### Metal fabrication and welding

Join and assemble material into usable structures. Fabrication work follows drawings and procedures, then checks whether the finished work meets its requirements.

Typical tasks:

- Read dimensions and prepare components.
- Use the specified joining process and sequence.
- Inspect the assembly and communicate any deviation.

**Works with:** Designers, machinists and quality inspectors.

**Classroom connection:** Material properties and heat affect the result of a joining process.

**Company evidence:** Employer career material lists welding; application and exact location are unverified.

### Industrial machinery assembly and maintenance

Make machinery work together as a physical system. Millwright work connects assembly, alignment, inspection and troubleshooting.

Typical tasks:

- Read equipment drawings and plan assembly steps.
- Align and connect mechanical components.
- Check operation and record problems that need adjustment.

**Works with:** Designers, operators, electricians and fabrication teams.

**Classroom connection:** Small alignment errors can change how forces and motion pass through a machine.

**Company evidence:** Employer career material lists assembly/mechanical work; task descriptions are general.
