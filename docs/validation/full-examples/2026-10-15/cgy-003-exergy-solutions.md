## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A part can be made in more than one way.

An illustrative connection between classroom ideas and sourced work.

1. Start with what a part needs to do, including dimensions, loads and the conditions it will face.

2. An additive process builds material up; machining removes material from a workpiece. Exergy offers both metal printing and CNC machining.

3. Compare access to features, material choice, surface finish and the work needed after the initial shape is made.

4. Measure and test the result against requirements. A complicated shape is not automatically a better or more reliable part.

**Discuss:** When might a simple machined part be preferable to a complex printed one?

**Try thinking it through:** Use a fictional bracket with a mounting hole and a tight fit requirement. Ask students to choose a manufacturing route and list the evidence needed before accepting the part.

**Teacher note:** The bracket is an illustrative problem, not a claimed Exergy project. Good answers connect the choice to a requirement and include inspection.

**Subject connection:** Physics: forces and materials; applied mathematics: geometry and tolerance. Course mapping remains provisional.

**Evidence distinction:** Company process statements come from the sources above. The teaching interpretation, fictional activity and discussion are editorial. No fictional values are company measurements.

## Roles tab — proposed copy

The profiles explain kinds of work. The company-evidence note under each profile states how strongly it is linked to this organization. None of these profiles is a promise of a vacancy.

### Mechanical design and prototyping

Translate a need into a component or machine that can be built and tested. Designers use dimensions, material properties and constraints to evaluate alternatives.

Typical tasks:

- Create and revise drawings or a digital model.
- Choose materials and dimensions for the intended conditions.
- Compare the prototype with its requirements and refine the design.

**Works with:** Machinists, fabricators, test staff and customers.

**Classroom connection:** Geometry, forces and material properties shape real design trade-offs.

**Company evidence:** Design engineering and reverse engineering are stated services.

### Machining and dimensional inspection

Make parts fit their intended purpose by controlling dimensions. Machining combines tools, setup and measurement; finishing a part is not the same as proving it meets its specification.

Typical tasks:

- Interpret a drawing and plan a sequence of cuts.
- Set up the machine and check the workpiece.
- Measure critical dimensions and document deviations.

**Works with:** Designers, production teams and quality inspectors.

**Classroom connection:** Tolerance describes an acceptable range rather than a perfectly exact number.

**Company evidence:** CNC machining is a stated in-house capability; exact job duties are explained generally.

### Quality and traceability

Make it possible to explain how a result or product was produced. Quality work checks records, methods and acceptance criteria so variation can be investigated rather than hidden.

Typical tasks:

- Check that materials and samples have traceable identities.
- Compare recorded results with defined acceptance criteria.
- Investigate deviations and document what changed.

**Works with:** Production, laboratory, engineering and customer teams.

**Classroom connection:** Repeated results need both measurements and records of the conditions that produced them.

**Company evidence:** Testing is part of the stated integrated facility; this is a work-area interpretation.
