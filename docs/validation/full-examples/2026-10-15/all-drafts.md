## Example card

**How can one seed become several useful ingredients?**

Useful ingredients are hidden inside an ordinary seed.

**Local note:** Calgary headquarters and R&D; the sources do not establish that every manufacturing step takes place in Calgary.

## Company tab — proposed copy

Botaneco develops ingredients from oilseeds for feed, food and personal-care products. It describes a water-based process that separates proteins and oleosomes, the seed’s natural oil-containing structures. Its Calgary headquarters includes research and development staff; its team names biochemists, engineers, food scientists and technicians.

**The problem:** How do you separate useful components while retaining the properties a customer needs?

**What it offers:** Oilseed ingredients and a processing approach designed to preserve their functionality.

**Who uses the work:** Ingredient users developing aquaculture feeds, plant-based foods and personal-care products.

**Evidence:** [Company and Calgary team](https://botaneco.com/about/); [Processing platform](https://botaneco.com/technology/); [Products and uses](https://botaneco.com/); [Careers check](https://botaneco.com/careers/)

## Classroom tab — proposed copy

Useful ingredients are hidden inside an ordinary seed.

An illustrative connection between classroom ideas and sourced work.

1. A seed contains several kinds of material. Botaneco works with oilseeds including canola and sunflower.

2. The company describes an aqueous process for separating proteins and intact oleosomes while avoiding harsh processing conditions.

3. Different recovered components can serve different ingredient uses. Separating them is only part of the challenge; their behaviour in a product also matters.

4. A useful evaluation compares the amount recovered, the properties retained and the resources used. A company’s sustainability claim needs its own supporting evidence.

**Discuss:** If a process recovers more material but changes how it behaves, has it improved?

**Try thinking it through:** Give groups three fictional ingredient samples: one has high recovery, one stays mixed longer, and one needs less processing energy. Ask each group to choose a sample for a product and identify the missing test.

**Teacher note:** There is no universal winner. Students should state the product requirement before comparing evidence. Do not treat the fictional samples as Botaneco results.

**Subject connection:** Chemistry: mixtures and properties; Biology: molecules in living systems. Specific course/outcome mapping remains provisional.

**How can magnets help identify what is in a sample?**

A chemical sample can produce a pattern we can interpret.

**Local note:** Calgary head office and instrument-development/manufacturing company; individual team assignments are not mapped to a specific vacancy.

## Company tab — proposed copy

Nanalysis develops and manufactures compact magnetic-resonance instruments and identifies its head office in Calgary. Its benchtop NMR products are used in areas including teaching, chemistry and food science. This example focuses on chemical analysis rather than medical imaging.

**The problem:** How can an instrument reveal information that cannot be obtained by looking at a sample?

**What it offers:** Benchtop nuclear magnetic resonance instruments and support for their applications.

**Who uses the work:** Laboratories, researchers, educators and industrial users analysing materials.

**Evidence:** [Company, products and Calgary address](https://www.nanalysis.com/); [How the signal becomes a spectrum](https://www.nanalysis.com/nmready-blog/2023/6/27/relation-between-the-fid-and-the-nmr-spectrum); [Careers check](https://www.nanalysis.com/career)

## Classroom tab — proposed copy

A chemical sample can produce a pattern we can interpret.

An illustrative connection between classroom ideas and sourced work.

1. Place a suitable sample in the instrument’s magnetic field. NMR concerns the magnetic behaviour of particular atomic nuclei.

2. A radiofrequency pulse changes the sample’s magnetization. The instrument records a time-varying signal.

3. Processing converts the recorded signal into a spectrum. The pattern can provide information about chemical environments.

4. Interpret the spectrum alongside reference information and sample conditions. A simplified pattern is not a photograph of a molecule or an automatic proof of identity.

**Discuss:** What additional evidence would you want if two samples produced similar patterns?

**Try thinking it through:** Show two invented spectra with a shared peak and one different peak. Ask students what they can conclude, what they cannot conclude, and which reference measurement would help.

**Teacher note:** This is a pattern-and-evidence activity, not instruction in assigning real NMR spectra. Avoid describing the signal as X-rays or nuclear radiation.

**Subject connection:** Physics: fields, waves and measurement; Chemistry: chemical identification. Proposed extension, not a verified curriculum outcome.
